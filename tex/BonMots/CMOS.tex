\section{CMOS Chips}  \label{section:CMOSChips}

Remember we want to count photons. Good CCD cameras have a well-depth in excess
of 65535 and require the accumulated charge to be attenuated. This factor
was/is recorded with the GAIN keyword. Most raw images are 16-bit representation
of the total value.

CMOS cameras typically have well-depths less than 65535 photons. Thus CMOS
GAIN should be 1.

CMOS chips are different from CCD chips significant ways. In most science cameras,
each pixel is a gate sequence that is shunted to a buffer/amplifier for each column.
This avoids the ``bucked brigade'' (row shift/transfers) common to CCDs. However
this make the keyword GAIN behave in a weird way due to the behavior of that
column infrastructure. Ideally CMOS gain should be an array.

The keyword GAIN \index{IRAF!GAIN} \index{CMOS!GAIN} has significance in IRAF.
CMOS cameras report a GAIN value for the total output of the system together with
an OFFSET. These keywords should be changed to CMOSGAIN and CMOSOFFS to move
the values to the side. Set the IRAF GAIN to 1.0.

The capture and saving of CMOS data follows roughly:

\begin{itemize}
\addtolength{\itemsep}{-0.5\baselineskip}
   \item   Pixels are cleared at the start of the exposure. A latching buffer
     captures the start level of the pixel. 
   \item As the exposure proceeds, electrons are accumulated. One
     aspect of GAIN occurs in this step.
   \item When the exposure stops, a second buffer latches the new value. The
     difference between the two are taken and a second aspect of GAIN occurs.
   \item  That value is held, the chip reads out each pixel by gating its value
     onto a column bus - this feeds into an AMP at the end of the column, then int
     an ADC (Analog to Digital Converter) with a third aspect of GAIN.
   \item  The ADC outputs a 10/12/14/16 bit value that is buffered, digitally in
     camera memory. The next exposure can start, and the computer can read this data
     while the new exposure is formed.
   \item   A pixel is a very complex structure:
\begin{itemize}
\addtolength{\itemsep}{-0.5\baselineskip}
   \item   at least one, often many, ADC
   \item   clock generators and power supply regulators
\end{itemize}
   \item   on-die processing
   \item   secondary processor
   \item   DDR buffer
\end{itemize}

CMOS chips have different modes of operation.


\begin{quote}
When it comes to CMOS cameras, ``amp glow'' is usually not from an
amplifier. CMOS sensors are usually “fully integrated” which means
that, unlike a CCD, readout electronics are included on the sensor die
along with all the pixels themselves. Each sensor has at least one,
often many, ADC (analog to digital conversion) and CDS (noise
reduction) units on it. There are also other support circuits on the
sensor die itself these days ... clock generators and power supply
regulators and such. These support circuits can generate heat or may
even emit NIR light, both of which can cause glows. Additionally, many
modern CMOS sensors include high performance image processing as part
of the sensor package, either in the form of on-die processing or a
secondary processor that is directly integrated into the sensor by
attaching it (often to the reverse side of the sensor.) This
processing circuitry can often generate heat that may produce glows.

\hskip{2cm }\url{https://astronomy-imaging-camera.com/faq}
\end{quote}
