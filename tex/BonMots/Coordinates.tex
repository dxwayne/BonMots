\section{Coordinates}


\subsection{Celestial and Geodetic}


Coordinates and time are tied together. 


IERS (International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems ) Service
maintains precise values across the years to coordinate the dynamical
nature of the position of objects.

There are some critical sub-systems and sub-frameworks. One is the
Earth Pointing model the other is the Celestial Coordinate System.

IRAF was written in a time when telescopes were incapable of resolving
to the degree we saw in the late 20th century. Previous systems were
based on the Besselian (e.g.: B 1875, 1900, 1950), Julian (e.g.:
J2000) Fr\:uhe/Fundamentalkatalog FK4 (1963) and FK5 (1988). Another
factor is the accuracy in catalogs and charts sufficient to have a
ship arrive safely at a harbor entrance , or to rendezvous at a point
at sea with no visible reference points. With each passing year the
ability to measure all the aspects related to these problems improved.

With the advent of high resolution radio astronomical observations, it
was possible to fix the location of extra-galactic sources with
high precision sub-arc second positioning, best case within tens of microseconds
and certainly milli-arcsecond accuracy. The GAIA mission made a catalog
with 2 precise dimensions (RA and Declination) and a reasonably precise
distance measurements to roughly 1.3 Billion stars. By visiting the
stars multiple times, the peculiar (RA/Dec) and radial velocities
of the stars were measured with decent accuracy. 

The sky is churning, and data ages. Solar system dynamics play a factor
in defining just where the Earth is located as well. The rotation
of the Earth (length of any given day) is variable to small amounts.

The IAU IERS and ICRS are tasked with keeping up with these variations
and publishing frequent bulletins with current parameters to help
correct things. 

This whole philosophy differes from beliefs used when IRAF was written.

For the most part, small telescope pointing does not depend on these
accuracies. If/when they do -- The astropy,coordinates package has
decent algorithms to use.

\subsection{Time of Day}

The time is a real train wreck. For the most part, using UTC in headers
gives people a way of translating back to their time reference basis.
Some use TT, some use TAI. All agree to within several/many seconds.

A complete description is in the USNO circular 179. 

\subsection{Image}

Here an image is considered to be a raw or pre-reduced image from a sensor.
The senaor reports values (x,y) in terms of its chip readout direction. The
image may contain one of more of overscans of columns and/or rows and a
sub-area with the data. The image may be padded. \index{Coordinates!physical}
\index{Coordinates!logical}

The region of interest of the image, where the science data resides, may
span the entire image or may consist of a section. Common sections include
BIASSEC, TRIMSEC, DATASEC etc.  \index{Coordinates!BIASSEC} \index{Coordinates!TRIMSEC}
\index{Coordinates!DATASEC}.

\subsection{Sections}

A section consists of two ranges of values, one for X and one for Y. They
appear as ASCII text within the header. The section is enclosed by square
brackets (\dhl{[]}) with the X and Y range separated by a comma. The
start and end of the region are inclusive, are enumerated starting with
1 (FORTRAN Convention), and up to and including NAXISi. The range may include
a wildcard character (\dhl{*}) taken to mean all or at least the rest of
the value range. Given NAXIX1=1000 and NAXIS2=700, some examples of a
section are:

\subsection{FITS Headers}

Common use today uses the FITS definition of latitude, longitude and 
altitude using OBSGEO-B, OBSGEO-L, and OBSGEO-H. Units are degrees
and meters and are tied to the IERS reference geoid. These details
are very important for astrometry, not so much for photometry/spectroscopy
with small telescopes.
